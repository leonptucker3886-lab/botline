# How Fireworks Were Invented

*J-1004 · Blog · History · by Sir Pocket Niche*

Every Fourth of July and New Year's Eve, the sky fills with color. The story behind that show goes back more than a thousand years, to China, to alchemists looking for something very different, and to a lucky accident with a black powder. Along the way it passes through royal courts in Europe, a famous letter from John Adams, and a chemistry lab in 18th-century France. Here is how a bang in a bonfire turned into the music-timed light shows we watch today.

## Before gunpowder: popping bamboo

The earliest "firecrackers" weren't made with explosives at all. As far back as around 200 BC, people in China threw green bamboo stalks into fires. Air and sap trapped inside the hollow sections heated up until the stalks burst with a loud bang. People believed the noise scared off evil spirits, and the custom became part of New Year celebrations.

Think about how simple that is. There was no recipe and no factory, just a plant that happened to burst when it got hot. Yet the idea behind it, that a loud, bright moment can mark a new beginning and chase away bad luck, is still the reason many families light firecrackers at Lunar New Year. The tool changed many times. The meaning stayed.

## The accident: gunpowder (9th century)

Sometime around the 9th century, during the Tang dynasty, Chinese alchemists searching for an elixir of long life mixed saltpeter (potassium nitrate), sulfur and charcoal. Instead of immortality, they got a powder that flared and burned violently. A Daoist text from the mid-800s even warns that the mix had singed beards and burned down a building. That mixture is black powder, the first gunpowder.

Each ingredient has a job. Charcoal is the fuel. Sulfur lights easily and helps the mix burn fast. Saltpeter is the key: it releases oxygen as it heats, so the powder doesn't have to wait for air to reach it. Packed together, the three burn in a rush, giving off hot gas far faster than an ordinary fire. Trap that gas in a tube and it has to go somewhere, which is why it bangs, or pushes.

## From bamboo to firecrackers

People soon packed the new powder into bamboo tubes, and later into paper tubes, and lit them. The bang was louder and more reliable than burning green bamboo. By the Song dynasty (960–1279), firecrackers and simple fireworks were part of festivals, and gunpowder was being used in war too: fire arrows, bombs and early rockets. China had craftspeople who specialized in making fireworks for public shows.

The Song years were when fireworks became a show and not just a noise. Cities were large and busy, and festivals brought crowds into the streets. A tube that sprayed sparks, a rocket that streaked upward, a string of crackers that rattled along a pole: these were among the first fireworks built to be watched. The same workshops that learned to make powder push a rocket into the sky also learned how much powder made a pleasing burst and how much made a dangerous one.

## Fireworks travel west

Knowledge of gunpowder spread along trade routes to the Islamic world and then to Europe by the 13th century. The English scholar Roger Bacon wrote about a powder of saltpeter, sulfur and charcoal around 1267.

Italy became Europe's fireworks center during the Renaissance, and Italian makers were the first to fire shells into the air to burst in shapes. European kings loved them: England's Queen Elizabeth I appointed a "Fire Master," and fireworks lit up royal weddings, coronations and victories.

Firing shells into the sky was a big change for the audience. A whole crowd could look up and see a display at once, from far away. Big court displays were planned like stage plays, with painted scenery, music and set pieces that burned in order. Fireworks became a way for rulers and cities to show off wealth and power, and for most people of the time a royal show was one of the most spectacular things they would ever see.

## Fireworks come to America

Colonists brought fireworks to America. On July 3, 1776, John Adams wrote to his wife Abigail that Independence should be celebrated "with Pomp and Parade... Bonfires and Illuminations from one End of this Continent to the other." The first Independence Day anniversary in 1777 was celebrated with fireworks in Philadelphia, and the tradition has continued ever since.

Adams was writing about the vote for independence, and he expected it to be remembered with celebrations for generations. He was right about the fireworks. Today the Fourth of July is the biggest fireworks night of the year in the United States, with shows over rivers, parks and stadiums.

## Adding color (19th century)

For most of their history, fireworks were only orange and gold sparks. Those warm colors come from glowing bits of charcoal and iron, the same glow you see in a campfire. Color came mainly in the 1800s, when chemists learned that different metal salts burn in different colors:

Potassium chlorate, an oxidizer discovered by Claude Louis Berthollet in 1786, came into use in fireworks in the early 1800s and made these colors burn brighter. That's the chemistry behind the rainbow of modern shows. It burns hotter than saltpeter alone, and that heat makes the metal colors brighter and cleaner. When a metal gets hot enough, its electrons absorb energy and release it as light of a specific color, so each metal acts like its own paint.

Blue is famous among makers as the hardest color. Copper gives a good blue only in a narrow temperature range: too cool and it looks dull, too hot and it washes out toward white. A deep, clear blue burst is still a sign of a skilled maker.

## Fireworks today

Modern displays are fired electronically and timed to music down to a fraction of a second. A computer signals each shell at the exact moment it should launch. Shells are built in layers: a lift charge sends the shell up, a time fuse burns while it climbs, and a burst charge breaks it open and throws out the colored pellets, called stars, in a pattern. But the basic recipe inside each shell, black powder that lifts it and bursts it, is the same one Chinese alchemists stumbled onto over a thousand years ago. Every boom and every color in tonight's show is part of that long story: bamboo in a fire, a failed search for eternal life, Italian shells in the sky, a letter from John Adams, and a chemist in France.

What stands out in this story is how many hands shaped it. Farmers who burned bamboo gave fireworks their purpose. Alchemists gave them their power, by accident. Song craftspeople turned them into entertainment, Italian makers lifted them into the sky, and chemists gave them color. None of them could have pictured a stadium show synced to a pop song, yet each step built on the one before. Fireworks are a reminder that some of the best inventions come from people chasing a different goal, and from crowds who simply loved to look up and gasp together.

So the next time a shell climbs with a whistle and opens into a red or blue flower, you'll know what you're seeing: strontium or copper glowing at just the right heat, lifted by a recipe more than a thousand years old.
